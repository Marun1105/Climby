Climby's next gains are not in the sign-up form any more. They sit in four places: the camera timer, the 30–40-second wait for the first answer, the "full solution on request" button, and a handful of defaults (dark theme, seven-stop tour, streak with no off switch) that cut against the best available evidence. The single highest risk is legal, not visual: inferring a student's attention or emotion from a camera in an education setting has been a prohibited AI practice in the EU since 2 February 2025, so the stored "presence percentage" must stay a plain count of time at the desk and must never be labelled, computed or shared as focus. The single largest usability defect is the wait, which is three to four times past the 10-second limit for holding attention; it needs a real progress indicator, a stated time estimate, and the page transcription shown as soon as it exists. The tutoring design is right in principle, but twenty years of tutoring research says an answer button alone gets clicked through, while a 2026 trial shows a tutor that only refuses goes unused, so the answer should unlock after a real attempt. Most of what Climby already does for motivation (one forgiven day, points that buy nothing, no chat, numbers-only adult views) matches the evidence and should be kept. The honest limit of this report: almost none of the evidence was gathered on Bulgarian students or on 12–18-year-olds using AI tutors, several legal texts were read only through law-firm or NGO summaries, and each recommendation below says so where it applies.

**How to read the tables.** Priority: **P1** do first, **P2** next, **P3** when convenient. "Applies?" judges each item against the product facts supplied. Evidence labels: *Law* (binding), *Guidance* (official but non-binding), *Experiment* (controlled study), *Usability* (usability testing or expert-body guidance), *Vendor* (company data), *Opinion* (practitioner view or the researchers' own inference). **2nd-hand** means the primary text was not read and the claim comes from a summary, abstract or search snippet; **recalled** means it comes from the researcher's memory of a well-known document that was not re-opened. Anything so marked should be checked before it is quoted elsewhere.

This report extends the earlier note of 15 November 2026 on sign-up length, skippable onboarding and password rules; those points are taken as done and not repeated.

## The camera, consent at 14 and friends' visibility are the three red lines

The EU AI Act bans AI systems that infer emotions in education institutions, and the Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices reportedly give "inferring the interest and attention of students" as an example ([EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-part-ii)). Whether a consumer homework app counts as an "education institution" context is unsettled: the same guidelines reportedly place a commercial online language course outside the ban, while the Dutch data protection authority reads the scope to include online and distance learning ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/)). Pure presence detection is most likely outside the definition, because readily apparent movements are not emotion recognition unless used to infer emotions. Climby has teacher views, which moves it closer to the broad reading. The safe design is therefore to treat "present / not present" as the hard ceiling of what the camera ever computes.

The Commission's guidelines under Article 28 of the Digital Services Act probably do not bind Climby directly: the article covers "online platforms" that publicly disseminate user content, and micro and small enterprises are exempt ([5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)). They remain the clearest statement of what EU authorities consider safe defaults for minors, and they are used here as a benchmark. Both points are questions for a lawyer, not settled conclusions.

## A 30–40-second wait needs a progress bar, an estimate and an early result

Ten seconds is the limit for keeping a person's attention on a task; past it, users want to do something else and need to know when the system will finish ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). Climby's first answer takes three to four times that. Staged status messages are the right instinct, and an experiment on showing the work being done supports them, but every expert source reviewed asks for more at this length: **a percent-done or step-count indicator, a rough time estimate, and a way to stop** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)).

The earlier note said streaming the answer would do more than any redesign. The fuller evidence is weaker than that. **No controlled study was found that compares streamed with all-at-once answers** on comprehension, learning or trust, and the percentages quoted in blogs have no source. The one relevant experiment, on adults, found that behaviour was largely unchanged across 2, 9 and 20 seconds to the first word, and that the fastest answers were rated less thoughtful ([Tan et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)). For a tutor, raw streaming also scrolls later steps past a student who was meant to try first. The better target is to break one long wait into several short ones, starting with the transcription of the page.

## An answer button alone gets clicked through, and a wall gets abandoned

Hints-first with solutions on request is the right default. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students, an unrestricted chatbot raised practice scores by 48% and then left students **17% worse on the unassisted exam**; a guard-railed tutor avoided the harm but produced no gain ([Stanford SCALE summary](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/generative-ai-can-harm-learning); figures second-hand, the journal text was not accessible). The older tutoring literature shows what happens when the answer sits one click away: in one study **70% of help requests in the control group were clicks straight through to the final answer** ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/digital-tutor-nudges-students-to-slow-down-and-seek-help/2011/04), second-hand). The opposite failure is equally documented. In a 2024–2026 trial in 18 Tennessee middle schools, students who could not get an answer from Khanmigo largely stopped using it, and Khan Academy has since moved to giving gentle hints before an attempt and direct help after a wrong one ([KQED/Hechinger](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/66662/students-didnt-get-answers-from-khanmigo-they-didnt-want-its-questions-either), journalism about an unread paper; [Khan Academy](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21419), the vendor's own account). For a consumer app the cost of a wall is that students go to an unrestricted chatbot. The design goal is a ladder.

The most concrete checklist for this area is the UK Department for Education's product safety standards for generative AI. They are UK guidance for products supplied to schools and **are not law in Bulgaria**; they are used here because nothing as specific exists for the EU.

## Keep the streak and points, but add an off switch and drop loss framing

Climby's motivation design already avoids the patterns with the worst evidence. Points that never buy answers avoid the performance-contingent reward that a meta-analysis of 128 experiments found undermines intrinsic motivation ([Deci et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627), recalled). The forgiven day matches Duolingo's own finding that slack raised daily activity without more skipping ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-streak-research/), company-reported). What remains is the moment a streak breaks, which is where motivation drops ([INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-the-key-is-keeping-them-alive-19011), adults, second-hand), and the regulatory direction. The Article 28 guidelines list streaks among features to be off by default for minors, though several summaries say "communication streaks" and the primary wording was not read ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors)). The Digital Fairness Act, proposed on 11 November 2026 and **not law**, is reported to require an off switch for features that encourage compulsive use; the two news reports disagree on whether that covers all users or adults only ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/eu-digital-fairness-act-loot-boxes-dark-patterns); [gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2026/11/11/eu-proposes-digital-fairness-act-to-ban-dark-patterns-and-let-users-turn-off-streaks-and-autoplay/)). An off switch is the low-regret answer to both.

On reminders, the one randomised trial near this age group is a caution. Among 85 students aged 10–12, reminders raised practice on the day (69% against 60%) but the group without reminders studied on more days overall (26 against 22 of 36) and test scores were almost equal ([Nobbe et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11192903/), search-level reading; frequent irregular reminders, not one evening reminder). Teenagers already receive a median of 237 notifications a day ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day), US sample).

## Seven tour stops are three too many, and forms should forgive typing

The strongest evidence on tours is an experiment with 70 adults across four apps: those who read the tutorial succeeded no more often (91% against 94%) and **rated the tasks harder** (4.92 against 5.49 out of 7) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)). It tested static card decks on simple apps, so it argues most firmly against passive walk-throughs. Vendor data, read only as search snippets, points the same way on length: one benchmark gives median completion of 38% for tours of three to five steps and 25% for six to eight, and 69% for tours the user starts against 23% for tours that start themselves ([Produktly](https://produktly.com/research/saas-onboarding-benchmarks-2026)). These are adult business products, and finishing a tour is not learning. A new Climby student passes a welcome screen, sign-up, a code, four questions and seven tour stops before asking anything, which is a long way for an audience with "dramatically lower levels of patience" than adults ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/)).

For forms, the evidence is from adults, mostly in online shops. It is consistent across sources but should be read as likely, not proven, for teenagers.

## Dark by default and a "wider" reading layout both run against the evidence

For readers with normal vision, dark text on a light background gives better acuity and proofreading, and the advantage grows as text shrinks; the studies found no difference in fatigue, so "dark mode reduces eye strain" is not supported ([NN/g review](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)). The studies are on adults, none on teenagers, dyslexic readers or Cyrillic. Because people rarely change defaults, a dark default leaves most students on the weaker setting for sustained reading of small text.

On dyslexia, the supported measures are larger text, plain familiar sans-serif fonts, no italics and **shorter lines**. In high-school students with dyslexia, short lines raised reading speed by 27% with no loss of comprehension ([Schneps et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3776763), search-level reading). Special dyslexia fonts repeatedly show no benefit ([Wery & Diliberto 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233), search-level reading). A setting named "wider easy-reading layout" therefore needs checking: wider spacing and margins are supported, longer lines are not. For attention difficulties there is almost no experimental evidence on interface design at all; most advice is expert consensus from the W3C's non-binding cognitive accessibility note ([W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)).

## Conclusion

The pattern across all five areas is the same: Climby's instincts are sound and its defaults lag behind them. The product already refuses to sell answers, hide content behind points, expose children to strangers or show adults anything but numbers. What it has not yet done is hand the student the controls: a switch for the streak, invisibility for "studying now", a view of what a parent sees, a theme that follows the system, a tour that waits to be asked for. Each of those is small to build, and together they move Climby from "safe by intention" to "safe by default", which is the standard EU guidance now measures against, whether or not it formally binds a company of Climby's size.

Three things would change these recommendations and are worth settling first. A lawyer's reading of whether the AI Act's education prohibition and the DSA's platform rules reach a consumer app with teacher views fixes how hard items 1–6 must be held. The final text of the Digital Fairness Act will decide whether a streak off switch is courtesy or obligation. And five sessions watching Bulgarian students aged 12–18 use the photo flow would test the one assumption every table here leans on: that findings from adults, university students and English-speaking teenagers carry over. Nothing in the literature answers that, and no amount of further reading will.
