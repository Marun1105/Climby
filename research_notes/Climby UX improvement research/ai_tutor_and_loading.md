# AI tutor interface design and loading / perceived performance (Climby, students aged 12–18)

Evidence-strength labels used throughout:
**[CS]** controlled study / RCT / lab experiment · **[US]** usability or observational study · **[REG]** government or intergovernmental standard/guidance · **[VG]** vendor or expert-body guidance (NN/g, Google, Microsoft) · **[PO]** practitioner opinion · **(2nd-hand)** = I saw it only via a secondary source or search snippet, not the primary text.

Product facts assumed (from the brief): photo of textbook problem → strong model reads page (~30–40 s) → tutor model explains step by step, hints first, full answer only on request; floating chat; answers arrive all at once after a spinner with staged status messages (no streaming).

## 1. Perceived performance: response-time limits, skeletons vs spinners, progress indicators, waits of 30+ seconds

### Takeaway
A 30–40 s wait is three to four times past the 10 s limit at which users stop attending to the task; every authoritative source says a spinner alone is the wrong tool there. The wait needs a determinate (or step-count) progress indicator, a rough time estimate, real partial results as soon as they exist, and a way to cancel or do something else. Staged status messages are directionally right (the "labor illusion" experiment supports showing the real work), but they should be tied to real stages and paired with progress and an estimate.

### Cited Findings

**Response-time limits (foundational)**
- 0.1 s feels instantaneous; 1 s keeps the user's flow of thought uninterrupted (delay noticed, no special feedback needed between 0.1 and 1 s); 10 s is the limit for keeping attention on the dialogue. Beyond 10 s users want to do other things and need feedback on when the computer will be done. Based on Miller (1968) and Card, Robertson & Mackinlay (1991). **[VG, resting on older human-factors research]** — [Nielsen, "Response Times: The 3 Important Limits", NN/g (1993, updated 2014)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- For waits over ~10 s use a percent-done indicator: it reassures that the system has not crashed, shows roughly how long to wait, and gives something to look at. The 2014 update adds that anything slower than 10 s needs a percent-done indicator **and a clearly signposted way to interrupt**, and that such delays are acceptable only at natural breaks in the work. **[VG]** — [Nielsen, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- When the amount of work is unknown, show running progress in absolute terms (e.g. name each item as it is processed); a spinner is "a last resort". **[VG]** — [Nielsen, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- Nielsen in 2023 states the same three limits apply to AI tools; sub-second is ideal; over 10 s needs percent-done; 2–10 s a light "working" indicator. He also recommends for slow AI: deliver the most critical result first, show preliminary/partial results, allow asynchronous work (let the user enter the next prompt before the last finishes), and use dead time for tips or examples (non-intrusive, never autoplay audio). He cites Voicebot.AI averages from June 2023 (ChatGPT 4.0: 41.3 s; ChatGPT 3.5: 6.1 s) as examples of unacceptable waits. **[PO by a recognised expert; latency data 2nd-hand and dated]** — [Nielsen, "The Need for Speed in AI" (Aug 2023)](https://jakobnielsenphd.substack.com/p/the-need-for-speed-in-ai)

**Progress indicators**
- Always give immediate feedback; without a visible change users assume the action failed and retry. No indicator under ~1 s; looped animation (spinner) for ~2–10 s with explanatory text; **percent-done for 10 s or more**, plus an estimate of when it will finish, and let users stop the process. Static "Loading…" text should be replaced. Looped spinners on very long waits lead users to abandon or wait indefinitely. **[VG]** — [Sherwin, "Progress Indicators Make a Slow System Less Insufferable", NN/g (2014)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- Estimates: give a general rather than precise estimate and pad it (nobody complains about finishing early); a step count ("3 of 50") lets users judge duration without a percentage; start slower and speed up toward the end; users notice and resent a bar that races then stalls near the end. If wait times vary widely, lower the threshold at which you use percent-done. **[VG]** — [Sherwin, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- A University of Nebraska–Lincoln study (Nah 2004) found participants shown a progress indicator were willing to wait about three times longer than those shown none. **[CS, 2nd-hand via NN/g]** — [Sherwin, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- For children specifically: use percent-done indicators for slow loads, make them prominent enough that children notice them, and do not let interim animations obscure the indicator. **[VG]** — [Sherwin, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- Progress-bar styling changes perceived duration: in Harrison, Yeo & Hudson (CHI 2010), a bar with animated ribbing moving backwards and decelerating made waits feel about 11% shorter than a plain bar. **[CS, 2nd-hand: abstract/press, not full text]** — [Harrison et al., "Faster Progress Bars"](https://www.figlab.com/research/2010/faster-progress-bars); [author page](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars)
- Microsoft Fluent "Wait UX" guidance: if a wait exceeds three seconds, switch to a progress bar or show a content string reassuring that the process is active. **[VG, 2nd-hand via search snippet]** — [Fluent 2, Wait UX](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/wait-ux)

**Skeleton screens vs spinners**
- Under 1 s: neither. ~2–10 s: spinner for a single module, skeleton for a full-page load. Skeletons are for waits under 10 s; "Progress bars are strongly recommended for any page that takes longer than 10 seconds to load", and waits above 10 s need an explicit duration estimate. Skeletons are not for process-type waits (uploads, conversions) — use a progress bar or wizard. Shimmer/pulse animations can distract or cause accessibility problems. Skeletons do not replace real performance work. **[VG; thresholds are stated without data]** — [Tankala, "Skeleton Screens 101", NN/g (2023)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)
- Evidence on skeletons is thin and mixed. Mejtoft, Långström & Söderström (2018) user-tested a fictional news site and found the skeleton version scored higher on perceived speed and ease of navigation than the spinner version (sample size and effect size not verified). **[US, 2nd-hand: abstract only]** — [record of the paper](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsoai__edsoai.on1234588454). Contradicted by Viget's 2017 test, in which the skeleton screen produced the longest perceived wait of the three conditions; Viget concludes skeletons "aren't a silver bullet". **[US by an agency, 2nd-hand]** — [Viget, "A Bone to Pick with Skeleton Screens"](https://viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens)

**Showing the work during the wait ("operational transparency")**
- Buell & Norton (Management Science, 2011; five experiments on simulated travel-search and dating sites): showing the work being done (e.g. each airline as it is searched) raised perceived value versus a plain progress bar, and people could prefer the slower transparent service to an instant one with identical results; the transparent version was rated higher even with waits up to about a minute. Mediated by perceived effort and reciprocity. **[CS, 2nd-hand: abstract and summaries]** — [INFORMS record](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:57:y:2011:i:9:p:1564-1579)
- For LLM chat specifically, a CHI 2026 controlled experiment (Tan, Messerschmidt, Yin & Nov) varied time-to-first-token at 2 s, 9 s and 20 s: interaction behaviour was robust to latency; participants in the 2 s condition rated outputs as *less* thoughtful and less useful than at 9 s or 20 s; delays were mostly attributed to AI "deliberation", but very long waits sometimes tipped into frustration or doubts about reliability. Authors frame latency as a design variable with ethical implications. **[CS; sample size not confirmed; adults, not teens]** — [arXiv:2604.06183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)
- A small study (30 participants) of AI text-loading designs found animated/gradient loading indicators reduced subjective loading duration and scored higher on SUS. **[CS, small, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — ["Long seconds: how AI text-loading design affects the subjective feeling of waiting"](https://openaccess.cms-conferences.org/publications/book/978-1-964867-71-7/article/978-1-964867-71-7_16)

### Inferences (recommendations for Climby's 30–40 s page read)
- Replace the spinner with a determinate indicator. Because the read is a fairly stable 30–40 s, a time-based bar (padded to ~45 s, easing so it never stalls at 99%) plus a step list ("1 of 4: finding the problem on the page…") satisfies NN/g's "percent-done or step count plus estimate" rule. State the estimate up front in plain words ("usually about half a minute").
- Keep the staged status messages, but bind them to real pipeline events wherever possible. The labor-illusion and CHI 2026 results suggest a visible, honest account of work makes the wait feel worthwhile; fake stages would breach the DfE "no deception" expectation (section 7) and the trust-calibration advice in PAIR.
- Show real partial results the moment they exist: the student's own photo immediately (instant feedback under 0.1–1 s that the upload worked), then the transcribed problem text as soon as the vision model returns it, before the tutor's explanation. This converts one 40 s wait into several shorter ones and doubles as the "check what I read" step (section 4).
- Use the dead time productively and pedagogically: ask "What have you tried so far?" or "Which part is confusing?" while the page is read. This is Nielsen's "use dead time" and "asynchronous operation" advice, and it also collects the "genuine learner attempt" that the DfE standard and the tutoring research favour (section 3).
- Provide a visible cancel/retake control throughout, and let the student leave the screen (e.g. keep using the floating chat) and be notified on completion; a 30+ s wait is past the attention limit, so assume students will switch away.
- A skeleton screen is the wrong primary pattern for the 30–40 s read (NN/g limits it to under-10 s full-page loads). It is reasonable for ordinary screen loads of 1–10 s elsewhere in the app.
- Do not artificially slow fast replies in the floating chat on the strength of the CHI 2026 "2 s seems less thoughtful" result: it is one study on adults, and deliberate delay sits badly with child-safety expectations about deception.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests wait tolerance or progress-indicator designs specifically with 12–18-year-olds on a 30–60 s AI wait; the NN/g children's note concerns younger children and games.
- Optimistic UI: I found no authoritative research source in this pass (only general practitioner convention). It applies to Climby only for cheap, reversible actions (showing the sent chat message or uploaded photo immediately); it cannot be applied to the AI output itself.
- web.dev perceived-performance guidance was not fetched; nothing in these notes rests on it.
- Mejtoft (2018), Harrison (2010), Buell & Norton (2011) and Nah (2004) were seen only as abstracts or summaries; effect sizes should be checked in the papers before being quoted in a final report.

## 2. Streaming AI responses: streaming vs waiting, stop/regenerate, "what the AI is doing", mid-stream errors

### Takeaway
There is strong convention and solid response-time theory behind streaming, but I found no controlled study that isolates token streaming against all-at-once delivery on comprehension or trust; the widely quoted percentage figures are unsourced. The best direct evidence (CHI 2026) says time-to-first-token matters less to behaviour than assumed and that some delay reads as "thoughtfulness". For a tutor, streaming also has a pedagogical cost if it reveals later steps before the student has tried the current one, so step-gated delivery is a better fit than a raw token stream.

### Cited Findings
- Streaming is the industry default for chat: Google's Chrome team describes Gemini and ChatGPT displaying responses as the model generates them and publishes implementation rules. **[VG]** — [Chrome for Developers, "Best practices to render streamed LLM responses" (Jan 2025)](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/render-llm-responses)
- Implementation rules from the same source: append chunks to the DOM rather than reassigning `textContent`/`innerHTML` (which rebuilds everything on each chunk); use a streaming Markdown parser that appends rather than re-parses; treat model output as untrusted user-generated content and sanitise the **accumulated** output (dangerous markup can span chunks); if the sanitiser removes anything, stop rendering. A streaming parser may hold back ambiguous characters (e.g. a lone `*`). **[VG]** — [Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/render-llm-responses)
- Controlled evidence on first-token delay: at 2 s, 9 s and 20 s time-to-first-token (then a fixed streaming rate of 25 tokens/s), user behaviour was largely unchanged; faster responses were rated less thoughtful and useful; very long waits sometimes produced frustration and reliability doubts. **[CS]** — [Tan et al., CHI 2026, arXiv:2604.06183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)
- Showing partial/preliminary results early and prioritising the most important output first are recommended for slow AI tools. **[PO, expert]** — [Nielsen, "The Need for Speed in AI"](https://jakobnielsenphd.substack.com/p/the-need-for-speed-in-ai)
- A way to interrupt is required for any wait beyond 10 s. **[VG]** — [Nielsen, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/); letting users stop long processes — [Sherwin, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (CHI 2019; validated with 49 design practitioners against 20 AI products) include supporting efficient dismissal and efficient correction of AI output, which is the research basis usually cited for stop and regenerate/edit controls. **[VG backed by a validation study; guideline wording recalled, see section 6]** — [Amershi et al., Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)
- On failure, tell the user what the system could not do and why, give an alternative path forward, and hand control back to the user; build a report-a-problem route into error states and next to normal output. **[VG]** — [Google PAIR Guidebook, Errors + Graceful Failure](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/errors-failing/)
- Practitioner view on "what the AI is doing": LangChain argues streaming intermediate steps builds understanding, while a non-streaming/opaque interface requires established trust. **[PO, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [LangChain, "UX for Agents, Part 1: Chat"](https://www.langchain.com/blog/ux-for-agents-part-1-chat-2)
- NN/g diary study (425 interactions with ChatGPT, Bing Chat, Bard): more attempts needed per task indicates worse usability; participants tended to rely on the answers given and were annoyed by unnecessary or irrelevant information. **[US, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [NN/g, "Information Foraging with Generative AI"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/generative-ai-diary/)
- Unverified numbers to avoid: blog claims such as "68% rate 3 s+ responses as slow, 22% with streaming", "satisfaction up 40% after streaming", and an "n=200" perceived-time table have no cited source. **[PO, unsourced — do not cite as evidence]** — [example](https://99helpers.com/glossary/token-streaming); [example](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-20-latency-perception-gap-ai-interfaces)

### Inferences (recommendations for Climby)
- Stream the tutor's reply, but stream **one step at a time**: render the current hint/step as it generates, then stop and wait for the student ("Try the next line yourself", "Show next hint"). This gets the perceived-latency benefit (feedback within ~1 s instead of a second spinner) without scrolling the final answer past a student who was meant to attempt it first (see section 3 on hint abuse and the DfE "no full solutions by default" rule).
- Because the answer-on-request path exists, never stream the final answer as part of the default reply; keep it behind an explicit, slightly effortful control.
- Maths makes raw token streaming visually rough: half-written LaTeX renders as broken markup. Buffer to complete units (a whole formula, or a whole step) before painting, in line with the Chrome guidance that streaming parsers hold back ambiguous fragments.
- Controls: a Stop button visible the whole time something is generating (Nielsen's interrupt rule; HAX "efficient dismissal"); after completion, "Explain it differently" rather than a bare "Regenerate" icon, since a teen needs to know what a retry will change, and regenerating a maths answer that then differs undermines trust unless the difference is framed as a different explanation.
- Mid-stream failure: keep what has already been shown, mark clearly where it stopped ("I got cut off after step 2"), offer "Continue from here" and "Start again", and never silently discard or silently restart. This follows PAIR's "say what failed, give a path forward, return control" and Sherwin's point that users retry when they see no acknowledged state.
- "What the AI is doing": a short, truthful, function-worded status line ("Reading your photo", "Checking the numbers in part b") is enough. Avoid first-person inner-monologue ("I'm thinking hard…"), which conflicts with the DfE anti-anthropomorphism expectation (section 7).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing streamed with all-at-once delivery on reading comprehension, learning, or trust — for adults or adolescents. This is a real gap in the literature as far as this search could establish, not merely an unfetched source.
- No NN/g article specifically on streaming or on stop/regenerate controls was found; NN/g's AI-chat studies located here concern prompting, onboarding and information foraging.
- No authoritative guidance found on mid-stream error patterns specifically; the recommendation above is derived from general error-recovery guidance.

## 3. AI tutor pedagogy in the interface: hints-first, scaffolding, answer-giving, gaming the system

### Takeaway
The evidence backs Climby's hints-first design but shows its limits clearly. Unrestricted answer-giving AI raised practice scores and then lowered unassisted exam scores (Bastani et al.); a guard-railed tutor avoided the harm but did not produce a gain. Twenty years of tutoring-system research shows that students click through hints to the answer when the interface allows it, and a 2026 RCT of Khanmigo shows the opposite failure: a tutor that only asks questions goes unused. The interface therefore has to do two things at once — make answer-harvesting slightly effortful and visible, and make legitimate help quick and well timed (especially after an attempt or an error).

### Cited Findings

**Generative AI in maths classes**
- Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025, vol. 122 no. 26; published title "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics"): field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students. GPT Base (a standard ChatGPT-style interface) and GPT Tutor (prompts designed to safeguard learning), both on GPT-4. During assisted practice, scores rose 48% (Base) and 127% (Tutor) relative to control. On the later unassisted exam, GPT Base students scored 17% worse than students who never had access; GPT Tutor students were statistically indistinguishable from control — the harm was "largely mitigated", not turned into a gain. Students used GPT-4 as a "crutch". **[CS — field RCT; figures 2nd-hand: PNAS full text returned 403, numbers taken from the Stanford SCALE repository and RePEc abstract]** — [Stanford SCALE summary](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/generative-ai-can-harm-learning); [RePEc record of the PNAS article](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122); [SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- Kestin, Miller et al. (Scientific Reports, June 2025): randomised crossover trial, N = 194 Harvard undergraduates in an introductory physics course; a purpose-built AI tutor designed around the same pedagogical best practices as the active-learning class, and tailored to the specific lesson, produced significantly larger learning gains in less time (secondary reports: roughly double the gains; about 49 vs 60 minutes). Caveats: university students, two topics, no delayed test; authors say the tutor should not replace in-person teaching. **[CS; details 2nd-hand via summaries; population is not 12–18]** — [paper on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/); critique: [ETC Journal](https://etcjournal.com/2026/10/17/the-harvard-ai-tutor-study-eleven-months-later/)

**Khanmigo: what happens when the tutor refuses answers**
- Oreopoulos & Low (University of Toronto; NBER draft, Aug 2026): RCT with low-achieving students at 18 Tennessee middle schools, 2024–2026. Almost all students tried Khanmigo at first and many tried to get it to give the answer; when it refused and offered questions or hints, students largely stopped using it — "most students declined it most of the time." Khan Academy practice helped modestly; Khanmigo added nothing measurable. **[CS — RCT; 2nd-hand via journalism, paper not read]** — [Barshay, Hechinger Report / KQED MindShift (Sep 2026)](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/66662/students-didnt-get-answers-from-khanmigo-they-didnt-want-its-questions-either)
- Khan Academy's resulting design changes: the tutor is built into practice rather than a separate chat students must remember to open; **before** an attempt it gives only encouragement and gentle hints; **after a wrong answer** it steps in with more direct support (walks through the work, explains the error); it appears when students are stuck, make a mistake, or need help deciding what to try next. Khan Academy states the original "never give the answer" rule "needed more nuance". Pilot: about 5,100 students in five US districts. **[VG — vendor's own account, no outcome data]** — [Khan Academy blog, "Built in the Open" (Jun 2026)](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=21419); corroborated in [KQED/Hechinger](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/66662/students-didnt-get-answers-from-khanmigo-they-didnt-want-its-questions-either)
- Khan Academy markets Khanmigo to parents as offering "layered hints" rather than handing over the answer. **[VG, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=14335)

**Intelligent tutoring systems: hint abuse and gaming the system**
- Definition: gaming the system is "attempting to succeed in an educational task by systematically taking advantage of properties and regularities in the system used to complete that task, rather than by thinking through the material". Hint abuse (Aleven & Koedinger 2000) is a specific type; its link to poorer learning was established in 2000, and the general construct and its link to learning in Baker et al. 2004. **[Review by the originating researcher, read directly]** — [Baker, "Gaming the System: A Retrospective Look" (2011)](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/PSCS-gaming-v6.pdf)
- Baker et al. (CHI 2004): in the classrooms studied, no other off-task behaviour was associated nearly so strongly with reduced learning as gaming. **[US/correlational classroom study, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Baker et al., CHI 2004](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p383.pdf)
- Prevalence: in a Geometry Cognitive Tutor unit, students abused hints on 36% of actions (14% when rapid successive hint requests count as one action). **[US, 2nd-hand via snippet; original Aleven et al. 2006 not read]** — [LearnLab wiki, Help abuse](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse) (page returned 404 on direct fetch; figure from search snippet)
- Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger's "Help Tutor", which gave feedback on help-seeking itself: 70% of help-button presses in the control group were clicks straight through to the final answer, versus 48% with the Help Tutor; Roll reported 82% of hint users did not stop to read hints. **[CS, 2nd-hand via EdWeek]** — [Education Week (Apr 2011)](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/digital-tutor-nudges-students-to-slow-down-and-seek-help/2011/04)
- Not all gaming hurts: Baker et al. (2005) distinguish "GAMED-HURT" students from "GAMED-NOT-HURT" students whose gaming was not associated with poor learning. **[CS, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Baker, Roll, Corbett & Koedinger, AIED 2005](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/BRCKAIED2005Final.pdf)
- Gaming varies with tutor design: Baker et al. (2009) coded 79 differences between tutor lessons and found a design factor predicting a considerable share of variance in gaming; Baker characterises earlier remedies (Walonoski & Heffernan 2005; Baker et al. 2006; Arroyo et al. 2007; Roll et al. 2011) as "active and often disruptive" interventions, motivating redesign of the tutor itself instead. **[Review, read directly]** — [Baker 2011](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/PSCS-gaming-v6.pdf)

**Regulatory expectation that matches the research**
- UK DfE Generative AI product safety standards (updated 19 Jan 2026), Cognitive development: products should "not provide final answers, full solutions, or complete worked examples by default"; use progressive hints that prompt learners to attempt a step first; show full solutions only "after a genuine learner attempt"; add friction or teacher approval before switching to modes where full solutions are readily available; track and report cognitive offloading (revealing solutions, pasting answers, accepting large autocompletes). **[REG — UK, aimed at products supplied to schools]** — [DfE, Generative AI: product safety standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)

### Inferences (interface patterns for Climby)
- Hints-first with answer-on-request is the right default and matches both Bastani's guard-railed arm and the DfE standard. But Bastani's result is "no harm", not "gain", and the Tennessee RCT shows pure refusal drives students away (for a consumer homework app, to an unrestricted chatbot). Design for a graded ladder, not a wall.
- Gate the full answer on a genuine attempt rather than on a click. Options, weakest to strongest: (a) a confirm step ("Have you tried the hint?"); (b) a required attempt — type or photograph your working, or answer one small sub-question — before the full solution unlocks; (c) show the full solution with one step left for the student to complete. Option (b) is what the DfE wording asks for and what the hint-abuse literature implies, since a button alone gets clicked through (70% click-through in Roll's control group).
- Do not present hints as an obviously finite numbered stack ending in the answer ("Hint 1 of 3"). That is the exact affordance that produced click-through gaming in Cognitive Tutors. Make each further hint contingent on some response to the previous one.
- Avoid heavy-handed anti-gaming devices (long forced delays, scolding). Baker describes these as disruptive, and some fast hint use is harmless (GAMED-NOT-HURT). Prefer light friction plus design that makes thinking the easiest path.
- Give stronger help **after** an attempt or error than before one (Khan Academy's revised rule). In Climby: the first response to a photographed problem is orientation plus one hint; once the student submits working or a wrong answer, the tutor may be direct about where the error is.
- Put help where the work is. Khan Academy's move from a separate chat tab to help embedded at the point of difficulty suggests Climby's floating chat should open already scoped to the current step ("Stuck on step 2?") rather than as a blank general chat.
- Close each problem with a short unaided check (a similar problem, or "explain why step 3 works"). Bastani's core finding is that assisted practice performance badly overstates learning; a transfer check gives the student and the product an honest signal.
- Log and surface offloading (answers revealed without an attempt) to the student in a non-punitive way, and to parents/teachers if such roles exist; the DfE standard expects this reporting.
- Tell students why: a one-line, non-preachy rationale ("People who try first remember more — want a hint?") sets the expectation that Climby is not an answer machine, in line with HAX "make clear what the system can do".

### Gaps
- Bastani et al. primary text not retrieved (403). The specific safeguards in GPT Tutor (commonly reported as teacher-written hints and an instruction not to give the answer), the Turkish school setting, and the finding that students did not perceive the harm are widely reported but were **not verified** in this session.
- Khanmigo "design principles" as a single published document were not found; the notes rely on a 2026 Khan Academy blog post and press reporting.
- No controlled study found that tests specific LLM-tutor interface gates (e.g. required attempt vs. confirm click) against learning outcomes in 12–18-year-olds. The hint-abuse evidence comes from pre-LLM Cognitive Tutors, mostly in US schools.
- Google LearnLM / "learning science principles" material and Learning @ Scale / AIED LLM-tutor papers were not reviewed in this pass.

## 4. Showing the AI's reading of the photographed page: transparency, editable transcription, confidence, recovery

### Takeaway
There is no formal research standard for this step, but established product practice (Photomath) and general human-AI guidance (PAIR, HAX) agree: show what was read, next to the original, before or alongside solving, and make correction a one-tap edit. Numeric confidence scores are discouraged for general users; use categorical flags on the specific uncertain parts, or offer alternatives.

### Cited Findings
- Photomath's documented flow: point the camera, review what was recognised, edit if needed, then view steps; if a result looks wrong, compare the recognised problem with the original and tap the Edit (pen) button to correct it with the maths keyboard. **[VG — vendor documentation of shipped practice; 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Photomath Help, "Incorrect solutions and solving steps"](https://support.google.com/photomath/answer/14332812?hl=en)
- Confidence display (Google PAIR): first decide whether to show confidence at all — test whether it changes decisions. Categorical (high/medium/low) displays must "clearly indicate what action a user should take under each category". N-best alternatives suit low-confidence cases because "showing multiple options prompts the user to rely on their own judgement". Numeric percentages "are risky because they presume your users have a good baseline understanding of probability". Misleadingly high confidence "may cause users to blindly accept a result". **[VG]** — [PAIR Guidebook, Explainability + Trust](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)
- Aim for calibrated, not maximal, trust: "Tell the user when a lack of data might mean they'll need to use their own judgment"; tie explanations to the user's action; a partial explanation is usually best. **[VG]** — [PAIR Guidebook, Explainability + Trust](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)
- Error recovery (PAIR): what counts as an error depends on user expectations; context errors arise from wrong assumptions about the user's situation; on failure, say what could not be done and why, offer alternatives, and "let the user take over"; let users correct outputs; put feedback/report routes in both error states and normal output. **[VG]** — [PAIR Guidebook, Errors + Graceful Failure](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/errors-failing/)
- An interactive-correction pattern for OCR: no OCR is flawless on mobile camera input, so overlay candidate readings on the image and let the user tap the right one. **[PO, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Codesota, Interactive Correction](https://codesota.com/ocr/interactive-correction)
- Practitioner design guides recommend a confirm step showing the parsed equation in clean notation before solving, because a misread otherwise yields a confidently wrong answer, and keeping the recognised question beside the generated response. **[PO, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [vp0 blog](https://vp0.com/blogs/photomath-clone-camera-scanner-ui-swiftui); [ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com/articles/ai-homework-solver-app-onboarding-examples/)
- DfE standards require transparency to be prioritised in design and a real-time, age-appropriate notice when content is blocked. **[REG]** — [DfE standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)

### Inferences (recommendations for Climby)
- Add an explicit "Here's what I read" card: the rendered problem (typeset maths, not raw LaTeX) shown beside or over the student's photo, appearing as soon as the vision model finishes and before the tutor's explanation. It is the first partial result of the 30–40 s wait (section 1) and the cheapest point to catch a misread.
- Make it non-blocking but prominent: start tutoring automatically while showing "Not right? Fix it" — a blocking confirm on every problem adds friction for the common correct case. Use a blocking confirm only when the model itself flags low confidence or multiple problems on the page.
- Correction paths in order of effort: tap the right problem if several were detected (n-best / region selection); tap a highlighted uncertain token to choose between alternatives (e.g. "x²" vs "x³"); free edit with a maths keyboard; retake the photo. Each should re-run only what is needed.
- Confidence: no percentages. Highlight the specific uncertain symbols or regions ("I wasn't sure about this exponent") and say what to do. This follows PAIR's categorical-with-action rule and suits teens' probability literacy.
- Keep the "what I read" card pinned and reachable throughout the explanation, so that when a step looks wrong the student can check the reading first — Photomath's support guidance is exactly this comparison.
- When the read fails or is partial (blurry, cropped, handwriting, diagram), say specifically what was wrong and give the next action ("The bottom of the page was cut off — retake, or type the last line"), rather than a generic error.
- Pedagogical bonus: asking the student to confirm the problem statement is itself a small act of engagement with the task before any help arrives.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on editable-transcription or confidence UI for photographed maths problems, with students or otherwise. Recommendations rest on vendor practice plus general human-AI guidance.
- Mathpix's own confirmation/confidence UX was not found; a paper reports Mathpix as the most accurate conversion tool by BLEU/edit distance, which concerns accuracy rather than interface ([arXiv:2606.12976](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12976), 2nd-hand via snippet).
- No data found on how often teens actually check a transcription when one is shown.

## 5. Maths rendering and step-by-step explanations: chunking, progressive disclosure, worked examples, cognitive load

### Takeaway
Worked-example research supports step-by-step solutions for novices but, more usefully for Climby, supports **faded** examples: show early steps, leave later ones for the student, and reduce support as competence grows. This aligns the readability goal (small chunks, one step visible at a time) with the pedagogical one (the student does part of the work).

### Cited Findings
- Fading: Renkl and colleagues' approach starts with a fully worked example and progressively removes solution steps so the learner takes over; backward fading (removing the last steps first) showed positive learning effects in the cited studies. **[CS, 2nd-hand: reported in a 2006 engineering-education paper]** — [Moreno, Reisslein & Delgoda (2006), expertise-reversal / fading study](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- Adaptive fading in a tutoring system (Salden, Aleven, Schwonke & Renkl 2010): adaptive fading led to better learning than fixed fading, which in turn beat plain problem solving. **[CS, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [Salden et al., Instructional Science (2010)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)
- Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003): the advantage of worked examples shrinks and can reverse as learners gain prior knowledge; fully worked steps become redundant for stronger students. **[CS/review, 2nd-hand]** — [Moreno et al. 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf); [Heriot-Watt record, "The expertise reversal effect and worked examples in tutored problem solving"](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/the-expertise-reversal-effect-and-worked-examples-in-tutored-prob/)
- Completion problems (Paas): learners given partly worked problems performed as well on tests as those given fully worked ones. **[CS, 2nd-hand via a wiki-style summary — verify before quoting]** — [Worked-example effect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)
- Limits: under some conditions worked examples are no more effective than problem solving (Ward & Sweller), and the classic findings come mostly from well-structured domains. **[CS, 2nd-hand]** — [Worked-example effect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)
- Regulatory match: DfE says no "complete worked examples by default" and progressive hints that prompt an attempt first. **[REG]** — [DfE standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)
- Rendering: streamed Markdown/maths should be parsed incrementally and appended; ambiguous partial markup should be held back rather than painted. **[VG]** — [Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/render-llm-responses)
- Users are annoyed by unnecessary or irrelevant information in AI chat answers. **[US, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [NN/g, "Information Foraging with Generative AI"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/generative-ai-diary/)

### Inferences (recommendations for Climby)
- One step per card, revealed on demand. Each card: a short plain-language sentence saying what is being done and why, then one typeset line of maths. This is chunking plus progressive disclosure, and it prevents the eye from jumping to the final line.
- Fade rather than fully work: when a full solution is requested, show the early steps worked and ask the student to supply the last step or the final value (backward fading), unlocking the rest after an attempt. This reconciles "full answer when asked" with the worked-example and DfE evidence.
- Adapt the amount of support: fewer and terser hints as a student succeeds on a topic; fuller worked steps for a student who is clearly new to it (expertise reversal). Even a simple "more detail / less detail" control is a start.
- Keep explanation text and the formula it refers to physically adjacent, and keep the original problem pinned on screen so the student does not have to scroll back and hold it in memory. (This applies the split-attention principle from cognitive load theory; I did not retrieve a primary source for it in this pass — see Gaps.)
- Typeset all maths (never raw LaTeX or ASCII fractions), render a formula only when complete, allow long expressions to scroll horizontally on small screens rather than shrink below legibility, and highlight what changed between consecutive lines.
- Keep hint text short. Long paragraphs in a chat bubble work against both the NN/g finding on unnecessary information and the limited attention of a student mid-problem.

### Gaps
- No primary source retrieved for cognitive load theory's split-attention, redundancy or signalling principles, nor a maths-specific worked-example review; the worked-example findings here are second-hand and several come from engineering or other domains.
- No research found on on-screen maths typography or step layout for adolescents on phones (font size, line length, colour-coding of changed terms).
- No accessibility guidance for rendered maths (screen readers, MathML) was researched.

## 6. Human-AI interaction guidelines (Microsoft HAX 18, Google PAIR, NN/g) applied to a tutor for minors

### Takeaway
The general guidelines map cleanly onto Climby; the ones that matter most for a minors' tutor are setting expectations about capability and fallibility, cheap correction and dismissal, explaining why, and graceful failure. Two general-purpose guidelines need tempering for children: "learn from user behaviour"/personalisation (data minimisation) and "match social norms" (do not drift into a humanlike companion persona).

### Cited Findings
- Microsoft's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (Amershi et al., CHI 2019) are grouped into four phases — initially, during interaction, when wrong, over time — and were validated through multiple rounds including a study with 49 design practitioners testing them against 20 AI-infused products. **[VG backed by a validation study]** — [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/); [HAX Toolkit](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/)
- The guideline list as published in that paper: G1 Make clear what the system can do; G2 Make clear how well the system can do what it can do; G3 Time services based on context; G4 Show contextually relevant information; G5 Match relevant social norms; G6 Mitigate social biases; G7 Support efficient invocation; G8 Support efficient dismissal; G9 Support efficient correction; G10 Scope services when in doubt; G11 Make clear why the system did what it did; G12 Remember recent interactions; G13 Learn from user behavior; G14 Update and adapt cautiously; G15 Encourage granular feedback; G16 Convey the consequences of user actions; G17 Provide global controls; G18 Notify users about changes. **[VG — NOTE: the full list could not be re-fetched in this session (pages returned no list / 403); G1, G15, G17 and G18 were confirmed by search results, the rest are given from the researcher's recall of the paper and should be checked against the PDF]** — [paper PDF](https://research.microsoft.com/%7Ehorvitz/Guidelines_Human_AI_Interaction.pdf)
- Google PAIR: calibrate trust rather than maximise it; set expectations from onboarding; explain in response to user actions; scale explanation to stakes; be careful with confidence displays. **[VG]** — [PAIR, Explainability + Trust](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/explainability-trust/)
- Google PAIR: errors are defined by user expectations; give paths forward; let the user take over; let users correct; collect feedback in error states and alongside output, and when users repeatedly reject outputs. **[VG]** — [PAIR, Errors + Graceful Failure](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/errors-failing/)
- NN/g: new users struggle to understand what gen-AI chat tools can do; broad starter prompts helped newcomers more than detailed prompt formulas, and guidance shown before users had noticed the chat interface arrived at the wrong time (qualitative study, 6 participants). **[US, small, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [NN/g, "New Users Need Support with Generative-AI Tools"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/new-AI-users-onboarding/)
- NN/g: across 425 logged interactions, users tended to rely on chatbot answers; more attempts per task meant worse usability; a participant noted thumbs-down feedback did not stop irrelevant suggestions. **[US, 2nd-hand via snippet]** — [NN/g, "Information Foraging with Generative AI"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/generative-ai-diary/)
- NN/g also publishes a taxonomy of conversation types with generative AI. **[US, title only seen]** — [NN/g, "The 6 Types of Conversations with Generative AI"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/AI-conversation-types/)

### Inferences — how each applies to Climby
| Guideline | Application for a tutor used by 12–18s |
|---|---|
| HAX G1 / PAIR mental models | First-run and empty states say what Climby does (reads a photo of a problem; helps you solve it with hints) and does not do (hand out answers by default; replace the teacher). NN/g: offer a few broad tappable starters in the floating chat, shown when the chat is opened, not before. |
| HAX G2 / PAIR trust calibration | Say plainly and repeatedly, not only in onboarding, that it can misread photos and make maths mistakes; put "check what I read" next to the output. NN/g found users tend to rely on answers, so calibration cannot be left to a one-off disclaimer. |
| HAX G3, G4 | Offer help at the moment of difficulty (after a wrong attempt, on the step in view), per Khan Academy's redesign; scope the floating chat to the current problem. |
| HAX G5, G6 | Encouraging, age-appropriate, non-patronising tone; no assumptions about ability from gender, language or school type. Temper G5 with the DfE rule against implying personhood (section 7). |
| HAX G7, G8 | One tap to ask for a hint; one tap to stop generation, close the chat, or dismiss an unwanted suggestion. |
| HAX G9 / PAIR "let users correct" | Editable transcription; "that's not what I asked"; "explain differently". Corrections should take effect immediately. |
| HAX G10 / PAIR n-best | When the photo is ambiguous or several problems are on the page, ask or offer choices instead of guessing. |
| HAX G11 / PAIR explanations | Each step states why, not just what; when the tutor withholds the answer, it says why in one line. |
| HAX G12 | Remember within the session what the student already tried and which hints were given, so help does not repeat. |
| HAX G13, G14 | Personalise cautiously and with minimal data (GDPR/children's-data context); do not change behaviour abruptly between sessions. |
| HAX G15 | Per-step "this helped / this confused me / this is wrong" rather than a single thumbs on a long reply; a wrong-answer report should visibly do something. |
| HAX G16, G17, G18 | Tell the student what "Show full answer" means for their learning record; provide settings (hint level, history deletion); announce changes to how the tutor behaves. |
| PAIR graceful failure | Specific, blameless error messages with a next step; never a dead end; the student can always carry on without the AI (type the problem, ask a teacher). |

- PAIR's Mental Models and Feedback + Control chapters were not fetched.
- NN/g has, as far as this search found, no published research on AI chat use by teenagers or on AI tutors specifically; the NN/g AI findings above come from adult studies and were seen only as search snippets.

## 7. Safety and expectation-setting for AI used by children (UNICEF, UK DfE, Common Sense Media)

### Takeaway
The UK DfE standards (updated January 2026) are the most concrete, checkable design brief available and bear directly on tutor UX: no default full solutions, function-based non-humanlike language, no engagement-extending tricks, break prompts and time limits, plain-language reporting, and age-appropriate notices. UNICEF supplies the principle-level frame (ten requirements). Common Sense Media shows how a third-party reviewer will judge the product: favourably for scoped, purpose-limited tutors with published risk processes, critically for maths errors, bias and privacy.

### Cited Findings

**UK Department for Education — Generative AI: product safety standards** (first published 22 Jan 2025; updated 19 Jan 2026 adding cognitive development, emotional and social development, mental health, and manipulation). Aimed at edtech developers and suppliers to schools and colleges. **[REG — UK; not EU law]** — [landing page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-expectations/generative-ai-product-safety-expectations); [full standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)
- Stated purpose: state clearly the intended purpose, target users (e.g. age) and learning focus; do not "exaggerate the impact or capabilities" of the tool; claims need transparent evidence.
- Filtering: "must effectively and reliably prevent users from accessing harmful or inappropriate content"; moderation persists through a conversation, covers multimodal input (relevant to photo upload), multiple languages, misspellings and abbreviations, and works on personal smartphones.
- Monitoring and reporting: give users a real-time, age-appropriate notice explaining blocked content; signpost support resources; plain-language reports; report on requests for cognitive offloading, on the nature (not content) of personal/emotional engagement, and on each learner's usage duration.
- Privacy: clear, age-appropriate privacy notice presented at regular intervals; DPIA; no use of personal data for model training without a confirmed lawful basis; (UK) ICO Children's code.
- Design and testing: prioritise transparency and child safety; test with diverse users including children before release.
- Cognitive development: not provide "final answers, full solutions, or complete worked examples by default"; progressive hints prompting an attempt first; full solutions only "after a genuine learner attempt"; friction or teacher approval before switching to modes where full solutions are readily available; track and report offloading.
- Emotional and social development / anthropomorphism: do not imply emotions, consciousness, personhood, agency or identity; use function-based language rather than "I think" statements; avoid names, avatars or characters suggesting personhood (except clearly framed, time-limited, pedagogically justified role-play); avoid phrases like "You can trust me"; keep prompts task-focused; do not cultivate personal relationships; remind users AI cannot replace human relationships (e.g. suggest asking a teacher or classmate).
- Time limits: default time limits and advisory break prompts; hard limits the learner cannot bypass, reset by a teacher or administrator; a clear warning such as "Stop for now" with healthy-use guidance.
- No artificial extension of engagement: no changing responses when learners try to leave; no persistent questioning without pedagogical purpose.
- Mental health: detect signs of distress (including night-time usage spikes and repeated refusal to end sessions); tiered response from soft signposting to a safeguarding flag; supportive, non-validating, non-pathologising language that always directs to human help.
- Manipulation: no flattery, deception, false confidence, peer pressure, guilt or fear, threats of loss, or inappropriate reward promises; rewards only if low-stakes and educationally justified (e.g. a completion badge); no design for prolonged use or revenue, no steering toward paid options, no mixing pedagogy with advertising, no dark patterns.

**UNICEF — Guidance on AI and Children, version 3.0** (December 2025; earlier versions 2020 and 2021). **[REG — intergovernmental guidance, principle-level]** — [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children)
- Ten requirements for child-centred AI: (1) regulatory frameworks, oversight and compliance; (2) safety for children; (3) children's data and privacy; (4) non-discrimination and fairness; (5) transparency, explainability and accountability; (6) responsible AI practice respecting human and child rights; (7) children's best interests, development and well-being; (8) inclusion of and for children; (9) preparing and skilling children for current and future AI; (10) an enabling environment for child-centred AI.
- Version 3.0 was updated for generative AI and adds a focus on AI companions used by children; it draws on a twelve-country study with children and caregivers and comes with a practical checklist, eight case studies and guides for parents and teens. **[2nd-hand for the supporting materials]** — [UNRIC summary](https://unric.org/en/unicef-guidance-on-ai-and-children/)

**Common Sense Media — AI ratings / risk assessments**
- Khanmigo received an overall 4-star rating (review dated around late 2023 / updated Aug 2024) and is listed as "Low Risk" in press coverage. Praised: clear guidelines for responsible AI development, a risk and mitigation management process, and limiting conversations to a specific set of activities as an effective way to limit risk. Criticised: occasional reinforcement of gender biases and stereotypes in testing; maths limitations (generative chatbots "perform best with fiction, not facts"); privacy caveats. **[Third-party expert review; 2nd-hand — the Common Sense page redirected and was not read directly]** — [Khan Academy blog on the rating](https://blog.khanacademy.org/four-stars-for-khanmigo-common-sense-media-rates-ai-tools-for-learning); [Common Sense risk assessment page](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/khanmigo); [TechLearning coverage](https://www.techlearning.com/news/navigate-ai-with-new-risk-assessments-from-common-sense-media)
- Common Sense's current methodology uses a five-level risk scale: Minimal, Low, Moderate, High, Unacceptable. **[2nd-hand via snippet]** — [methodology](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/methodology)

### Inferences (copy and UX for Climby)
- Expectation-setting copy, in plain teen-level language, at three moments: onboarding ("Climby is an AI. It helps you work problems out; it doesn't just give answers. It can make mistakes — check what it read and check its maths."); next to output (a persistent "AI can be wrong — report a mistake" affordance); and at the answer gate (why trying first matters).
- Voice: function-based and warm without pretending to be a person. Prefer "Here's a hint" / "The next step is…" over "I think" / "I'm proud of you" / "You can trust me". If Climby has a mascot or named character, the DfE standard treats humanlike names and avatars as a risk; a clearly non-human, tool-like identity is the safer reading. This is a product decision the report should flag, since "Climby" plus a floating chat already leans toward a persona.
- Praise: specific and effort-focused ("You isolated x correctly"), not flattery; the DfE lists flattery and false confidence as manipulative tactics.
- No engagement mechanics that extend sessions: no streak-loss threats, no "are you sure you want to leave?", no notification nagging. Completion badges are acceptable if low-stakes. Add gentle break prompts after long sessions and treat late-night heavy use as a signal for a wellbeing nudge.
- Keep the floating chat task-scoped. Off-topic personal or emotional messages get a brief, kind redirection and signposting to a trusted adult or local helpline, not an extended conversation. Common Sense explicitly credits activity-scoping as risk reduction.
- Redirect to people: periodically suggest asking a teacher or classmate, particularly when the tutor is uncertain or the student is repeatedly stuck.
- Blocked or unsupported requests get an immediate, specific, age-appropriate explanation rather than a silent refusal or generic error.
- Photo uploads need moderation too (multimodal filtering), plus a plain statement of what happens to photos (pages may contain names, faces, handwriting).
- Privacy notice in age-appropriate language, re-surfaced periodically rather than shown once.
- Do not overclaim in marketing or UI ("always right", "get better grades"): the DfE expects claims to be evidenced, and Bastani shows practice gains do not equal learning.
- Test with real 12–18-year-olds before release, as the DfE standard expects.
