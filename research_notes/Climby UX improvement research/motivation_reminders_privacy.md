## 1. Streaks, points, badges, leaderboards for adolescents: effects and safe designs

### Takeaway
Gamification gives small, real gains in learning (strongest and most stable for cognitive outcomes, weaker and unstable for motivation), but reward-heavy and publicly competitive designs can lower intrinsic motivation over a term, and streaks work mainly through loss aversion, so a broken streak demotivates. Climby's current choices (one forgiven day, points that buy nothing, no public ranking) match what the evidence supports; the remaining risk sits in how streak loss and friend comparison are framed and notified.

### Cited Findings
- Sailer & Homner, "The Gamification of Learning: a Meta-analysis", Educational Psychology Review 32, 77–112 (online Aug 2019, print 2020): 38 publications / 40 experiments; small positive effects on cognitive (g = 0.49), motivational (g = 0.36) and behavioural (g = 0.25) learning outcomes; only the cognitive effect stayed stable in the high-rigour subsample; game fiction and social interaction (competitive-collaborative rather than purely competitive or none) were significant moderators for behavioural outcomes. **[PEER] (search; g-values from a secondary summary, abstract statements confirmed in the repository listing)** — [Augsburg repository record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- Hanus & Fox (2015), Computers & Education 80, 152–161: 16-week classroom study with badges and a leaderboard; the gamified class showed less motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time and lower final exam scores than the non-gamified class, with intrinsic motivation mediating the exam effect. University students, one course, so transfer to 12–18 is an inference. **[PEER] (search)** — [dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ce/HanusF15)
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin 125(6): meta-analysis of 128 experiments; expected tangible rewards contingent on engagement, completion or performance reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation, while positive verbal feedback increased it. This is the core overjustification evidence behind self-determination theory. **[PEER] (recalled)** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)
- Silverman & Barasch (2023), Journal of Consumer Research: an intact streak raised the share of people choosing to continue an activity (about 66%) compared with a broken streak (about 58%); people would watch an advertisement to repair a broken streak; and people were less motivated to keep a streak when they knew it could be repaired. Adult participants. **[PEER] (search, via secondary write-ups)** — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-the-key-is-keeping-them-alive-19011)
- Duolingo's own streak article (Osman Mansur; the version served to me was the Japanese-language post dated 13 May 2024): raising the limit to two streak freezes at once increased daily active learners by 0.38% without increasing skipped lessons; a redesigned milestone animation made new learners 1.7% more likely to be active after 7 days; Duolingo's survey data says reaching a 7-day streak makes course completion 3.6 times more likely (correlational, company-reported); the article itself states that losing a streak lowers motivation and that the pull of loss aversion grows with streak length. It cites a 2018 Penn/UCLA study that goals with some slack motivate more than strict ones. The article does not discuss anxiety or notifications. **[IND] (fetched; company self-report, not peer-reviewed)** — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-streak-research/)
- van Essen & Van Ouytsel (2023), Telematics and Informatics Reports: Belgian early adolescents (mean age about 13.5); problematic smartphone use, fear of missing out and lower social-media self-control were associated with Snapchat streak engagement, with weak correlations; girls engaged more and longer. Cross-sectional. **[PEER] (search)** — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7e7e45c958c74b29ba203607b6f4a840)
- A 2025 survey study in an Egyptian media journal reports teens prioritising streaks over sleep or study and distress on streak loss. Survey, association only, lower-tier journal. **[PEER, weak] (search)** — [article](https://mjsm.journals.ekb.eg/article_422365.html)
- The Commission's Article 28 DSA guidelines (14 July 2025) list streaks, with read receipts, autoplay and push notifications, among features that encourage excessive use and should be off by default for minors. Several summaries phrase this as "communication streaks". **[GUID] (2nd-hand)** — [Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors); [Commission page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)
- Digital Fairness Act proposal (Commission, 11 November 2026, not law): reported to require an option to switch off features designed or reasonably foreseen to encourage compulsive use, with streaks and autoplay named in one report (which names Duolingo and Strava as affected). **[PROP] (2nd-hand; the two news sources differ, see section 2)** — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/eu-digital-fairness-act-loot-boxes-dark-patterns); [gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2026/11/11/eu-proposes-digital-fairness-act-to-ban-dark-patterns-and-let-users-turn-off-streaks-and-autoplay/)
- ICO Children's code, standard 5 (detrimental use of data): advises against using personal data for features that extend engagement in ways that harm wellbeing (reward loops, continuous autoplay) and recommends pause mechanisms that let children take a break without losing progress. **[GUID, UK] (recalled)** — [ICO standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)

### Inferences
Recommendations (each is my synthesis; the sources behind it are named in brackets):
- **Keep points decoupled from answers and from any purchasable advantage.** This avoids the performance-contingent tangible reward that most reliably undermines intrinsic motivation [Deci et al.], and avoids the pay-to-progress pattern the DFA proposal targets for minors [TNW].
- **Reward effort and completion with informational feedback** ("you studied 4 days this week, 35 minutes longer than last week") rather than with controlling language ("don't lose your streak"). Positive informational feedback is the one reward type that raised intrinsic motivation [Deci et al.].
- **Keep the forgiven day, and make it automatic and free.** Slack increased activity without increasing skipping in Duolingo's data [Duolingo]. Never sell or gate it: charging to repair a loss the product created is the pattern critics call monetised anxiety, and people will pay or watch ads for repairs [Silverman & Barasch]. Note the trade-off: knowing a repair exists slightly lowers the streak's motivational force [Silverman & Barasch]; that is an acceptable cost for a minors' product.
- **Add planned rest days and a pause for holidays, exams and illness**, so a break is a choice with no loss [ICO standard 5; Duolingo's slack finding].
- **Soften the break.** When a streak ends, show the total ("42 study days this term") and the best run, and start the new run without a zero-shaming screen, because the broken-streak moment is where motivation drops [Silverman & Barasch; Duolingo].
- **Give the student a streak on/off switch, and consider a weekly goal (for example 4 of 7 days) as the default frame instead of an unbroken daily chain.** The Article 28 guidelines put streaks on the default-off list [Hunton] and the DFA proposal would require an off switch [gHacks]. Whether a study streak falls within the guidelines' "communication streaks" wording is unresolved, so a visible off switch is the low-regret design.
- **Keep comparison private or friends-only, and prefer self-comparison.** Leaderboards plus badges lowered motivation and exam scores in the one longitudinal classroom study [Hanus & Fox], while cooperative-competitive social designs performed better than none in the meta-analysis [Sailer & Homner]. For the friends screen, this argues for showing friends' weekly effort without rank order, and for adding shared or cooperative goals before adding any ranking.
- **Red lines:** no public leaderboard; no streak-loss countdowns; no paid streak repair; no points that unlock answers; no rewards for inviting friends or for turning on notifications, camera or sharing (see section 2).

### Gaps
- I found no randomised or longitudinal study of study-app streaks in 12–18-year-olds, and no peer-reviewed study that measures "streak anxiety" as a clinical outcome. The adolescent streak evidence is cross-sectional and about Snapchat.
- Sailer & Homner's effect sizes were not checked against the full text this session.
- Duolingo's streak-freeze churn figures circulating online (for example "21% less churn") have no traceable primary source and are excluded.
- I did not locate the 2018 Penn/UCLA "slack" study Duolingo cites.
- I did not find the primary guideline wording to settle whether "streaks" there means only messaging streaks.

## 2. Dark patterns and children: what regulators define and prohibit

### Takeaway
As of November 2026 the binding EU rules are general (GDPR fairness and data protection by design, DSA Articles 25 and 28 for online platforms, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive), while the specific child-design expectations live in guidance: the Commission's Article 28 guidelines (14 July 2025) name streaks, push notifications, autoplay and read receipts as default-off features, and the ICO code prohibits nudging children to give up privacy. The Digital Fairness Act, proposed on 11 November 2026, would turn several of these into directly applicable law but is at the start of the legislative process.

### Cited Findings
**EU Digital Services Act and the Article 28 guidelines**
- Regulation (EU) 2022/2065: Article 28(1) requires online platforms accessible to minors to put in place appropriate and proportionate measures for a high level of privacy, safety and security of minors; Article 28(2) bans profiling-based advertising to minors; Article 25 bans interfaces that deceive or manipulate users; Article 19 exempts micro and small enterprises from these platform obligations. **[REG] (recalled)** — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj)
- The Commission published the Article 28 guidelines on 14 July 2025 (cited as C/2025/5519). They are not binding, but the Commission says it will use them as a benchmark for assessing compliance with Article 28(1). **[GUID] (search/2nd-hand)** — [Commission page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors)
- Scope: platforms whose terms permit minors, services directed at or predominantly used by minors, and platforms whose providers know some users are minors (§6); micro and small enterprises are exempt under Article 19 unless designated very large platforms. **[GUID] (fetched via 5Rights analysis)** — [5Rights analysis, Aug 2025](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)
- Defaults (§57b): minors can interact only with previously accepted accounts; their activity is hidden from public view; others cannot download their content; geolocation, camera and microphone access are off by default; features contributing to excessive use, such as autoplay and push notifications, are off by default, particularly during core sleep hours. Platforms must not encourage minors to change protective defaults and must give child-friendly explanations and an easy way back to safer settings (§57–58). **[GUID] (fetched via 5Rights analysis)** — [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)
- Push notifications should be off by default and always off during core sleep hours; warning signals should explain consequences when a minor changes a setting; providers should avoid manipulative design and treat excessive use as a health risk; risk reviews at least annually with consultation of children and guardians; the Commission will review the guidelines "at the latest after a period of 12 months". **[GUID] (fetched via law-firm summary)** — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s)
- Summaries report the guidelines also name infinite scroll and "notifications artificially timed to regain minors' attention" as persuasive features to avoid. **[GUID] (search/2nd-hand)** — [Commission page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)
- Self-declared age is not considered sufficient where age assurance is needed; methods must be accurate, reliable, robust, non-intrusive and non-discriminatory, and whether age assurance is proportionate is itself to be assessed (§31). **[GUID] (fetched via summaries)** — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)
- 5Rights' criticisms of the final guidelines: little guidance on the differing needs of age groups under 18, and heavy reliance on companies' own best-interests assessments. **[IND] (fetched)** — [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)

**Digital Fairness Act (proposal)**
- The Commission proposed the Digital Fairness Act on 11 November 2026. Reported contents: a regulation replacing the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive; a general ban on dark patterns plus an annex of practices banned in all circumstances (ten reported, including false low-stock claims, pre-ticked add-ons, nagging pop-ups), extendable by the Commission; an option to disable features "designed, or reasonably foreseen, to encourage compulsive use"; bans for minors on paid loot boxes and pay-to-win or pay-to-progress mechanics; in-game currencies off by default for minors without parental approval; no profiling-based ads targeting minors; fines up to 6% of global turnover. It now goes to Parliament and Council. **[PROP] (fetched via news; primary text not seen)** — [The Next Web, 11 Nov 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/eu-digital-fairness-act-loot-boxes-dark-patterns)
- Source conflict: The Next Web says "adult users" get the option to disable compulsive-use features; the gHacks headline and search summary say all users can turn off streaks and autoplay. The treatment of minors (off by default? banned?) is not clear from either. **[PROP] (2nd-hand)** — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/eu-digital-fairness-act-loot-boxes-dark-patterns); [gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2026/11/11/eu-proposes-digital-fairness-act-to-ban-dark-patterns-and-let-users-turn-off-streaks-and-autoplay/)

**UK ICO Age Appropriate Design Code (in force 2 September 2020, enforced from September 2021; UK law, a persuasive benchmark for an EU product)**
- Standard 13: "Do not use nudge techniques to lead or encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data" or to weaken or turn off privacy protections. Nudge techniques are "design features which lead or encourage users to follow the designer's preferred paths". Services should not exploit unconscious psychological processes (colour, imagery) to this end, nor nudge children to lie about their age. Pro-wellbeing nudges are allowed: for ages 10–12, 13–15 and 16–17 the code recommends high-privacy defaults, explaining risk when a child tries to change them, nudging toward breaks, and providing pause-and-save tools. The page does not mention streaks. **[GUID, UK statutory code] (fetched)** — [ICO standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)

**EDPB**
- Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns in social media interfaces, final version 2.0 adopted 14 February 2023 (listed 24 February 2023). **[GUID] (search)** — [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-032022-deceptive-design-patterns-social-media_en)
- The six categories are overloading, skipping, stirring (including emotional steering), obstructing, fickle, and left in the dark; the guidelines say emotional steering has greater effect on children. **[GUID] (recalled; category list not confirmed this session)** — [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-032022-deceptive-design-patterns-social-media_en)

**FTC (US, not applicable in Bulgaria; shows regulator direction)**
- The amended COPPA Rule was published 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, with compliance due by 22 April 2026. The FTC did not adopt its proposed limit on push notifications and engagement techniques for children, calling it overly broad, but restated concern about using push notifications to keep children online in ways that could harm mental health; firms note Section 5 enforcement remains possible. **[REG/US + PRAC] (search)** — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa--rule); [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)

### Inferences
- **Is Climby inside Article 28 at all?** Article 28 applies to "online platforms", meaning services that store and publicly disseminate user-provided information. A study app with no chat and no user content, showing only numbers to approved friends, is arguably not an online platform, and a micro or small enterprise is exempt in any case. So the guidelines probably do not bind Climby directly. They are still the best available statement of what EU authorities consider safe design for minors, and GDPR and consumer law apply regardless. This is a legal question for counsel.
- **Climby-specific red lines derived from the above:** no loss-framed or guilt-framed copy ("you'll lose your 12-day streak", "your friends are ahead of you"); no notification timed to pull a lapsed user back; no reward for enabling notifications, camera, friends or parent sharing; no pre-ticked optional settings; no repeated prompts after a "no" (ask once, then leave the option in settings); equal visual weight for accept and decline; turning anything off must take the same number of steps as turning it on.
- A streak with one forgiven day, an off switch, and no loss notifications is defensible against all of the above; a streak that sends "last chance" pushes is the exact pattern the guidelines describe.

### Gaps
- Primary texts not read: Commission guidelines C/2025/5519 and the DFA proposal. All paragraph references and quotations for these are second-hand.
- The guidelines were due for review by about July 2026. I found no evidence of a revised version, but did not search specifically for it; treat the July 2025 text as the last confirmed state.
- DFA: application dates, SME treatment, and the exact rule for minors on streak-type features are unconfirmed.
- 5Rights' own design guidance (for example its "Pathways" and "Disrupted Childhood" reports) and the FTC's September 2022 report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" were not retrieved this session.
- Whether Bulgaria's consumer or data protection authorities have issued child-design guidance was not researched.

## 3. Notifications and reminders for teens

### Takeaway
Teens already receive a very high volume of notifications, the one randomised trial of study reminders in this age range found they helped on the day but reduced overall studying and did not improve test scores, and EU guidance expects push notifications to be off by default for minors and always off during sleep hours. Climby's single optional evening reminder fits this, provided it is opt-in, capped, neutrally worded, and never tied to streak loss.

### Cited Findings
- Common Sense Media, "Constant Companion" (2023; 11–17-year-olds, phone-tracking data): median of 237 notifications on a typical day; about a quarter arrived during school hours and 5% at night; median 43 minutes of phone use during school hours. US sample. **[IND] (search)** — [Common Sense press release](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day)
- Nobbe et al. (2024), npj Science of Learning, "Smartphone-based study reminders can be a double-edged sword": 85 students aged 10–12, 36 days of vocabulary practice; the reminder group got up to 16 reminders at irregular intervals. Probability of practising was 69% on reminder days versus 60% on non-reminder days, but the no-reminder group studied on more days overall (26 versus 22 of 36), and vocabulary test results were almost equal. The authors interpret this as reliance on reminders. **[PEER, randomised] (search)** — [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11192903/); [DIPF press release](https://www.dipf.de/en/dipf-news/press-releases/when-smartphones-remind-you-to-learn)
- A study of an English-learning app with about 12,000 users found push notifications can raise engagement but too-frequent notifications have the opposite effect. Design not confirmed as randomised. **[PEER] (search)** — [NCU record](https://scholars.ncu.edu.tw/en/publications/effects-of-push-notifications-on-learner-engagement-in-a-mobile-l/)
- Article 28 guidelines: push notifications off by default for minors and always off during core sleep hours; avoid notifications artificially timed to regain attention. **[GUID] (2nd-hand)** — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)
- FTC (2025): declined to regulate push notifications to children in COPPA but recorded continued concern about their mental-health effect. **[US regulator statement] (search)** — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-finalizes-amendments-to-coppa--rule)

### Inferences
Recommended defaults (synthesis of the findings above):
- **Opt-in, asked once, at a relevant moment** (after the student first sets homework), with a plain explanation of what will be sent and how often. No operating-system permission prompt on first launch.
- **One reminder per day at most, at a time the student picks**, sent only if today's work is not done. The Nobbe result suggests reminders support a plan the student made and should not become the trigger the student waits for; pairing the reminder with a student-set plan ("I study at 18:30") is the cautious design.
- **Hard quiet hours that the student cannot override**, for example nothing after 21:00 and before 07:00, and nothing during school hours. The guidelines do not define "core sleep hours" in the summaries I saw, so the exact window is a design choice.
- **Neutral, informative wording.** Acceptable: "Homework reminder: Maths, due tomorrow." Not acceptable: loss framing ("Your streak ends in 2 hours"), guilt ("We miss you"), social pressure ("Mira is studying now, are you?"), or false urgency.
- **No re-engagement pushes** to students who have stopped using the app, and no notification about friends' activity by default.
- **Auto-quieten:** if several reminders in a row are ignored, stop and ask in-app whether to keep them. This is practitioner logic, with no direct study behind it.
- **Parent and teacher messages follow the same rules;** a parent digest should be weekly, with no real-time alerts about a child missing a day.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed trial of evening homework reminders for 13–18-year-olds was found; the only randomised study is in 10–12-year-olds with irregular, frequent reminders, which differs from one fixed evening reminder.
- I found no study this session linking notification timing to adolescent sleep, though the search was brief.
- The guidelines' precise wording on notification frequency or content was not seen.

## 4. Social features for minors: friend finding, "studying now", comparison

### Takeaway
All four sources consulted point the same way: highest-privacy settings by default, contact only with accounts the minor has accepted, activity and status hidden unless the minor chooses otherwise, and easy blocking, muting and reporting. For Climby this means "studying now" and weekly points should be opt-in and visible to accepted friends only, for every user under 18 and not only under 14.

### Cited Findings
- Article 28 guidelines §57b: by default minors can interact only with previously accepted accounts, their activity is hidden from public view, and geolocation, camera and microphone are off. **[GUID] (fetched via 5Rights analysis)** — [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)
- Practitioner summaries of the guidelines list as expected defaults: limiting interactions to approved contacts, restricting visibility of account information, disabling contact syncing, and turning off visibility of likes and user activity; recommendations of other accounts off by default for child users; consent before a minor is added to a group; easy block and mute; accessible reporting with prompt feedback. **[GUID via PRAC] (search/fetched summaries)** — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors); [William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/deep-dive-on-new-child-online-safety-guidelines/)
- The final guidelines relaxed some default-setting expectations compared with the May 2025 draft. **[PRAC] (search)** — [William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/deep-dive-on-new-child-online-safety-guidelines/)
- eSafety Commissioner (Australia), Safety by Design: tools should be set to the most secure privacy and safety levels by default. In eSafety's survey of 3,520 parents of children aged 2–17, 68% wanted highest privacy settings by default, the top request. **[GUID + regulator survey] (search)** — [eSafety Safety by Design](https://www.esafety.gov.au/industry/safety-by-design); [eSafety blog](https://esafety.gov.au/newsroom/blogs/leading-the-way-with-safety-by-design)
- Australia's Children's Online Privacy Code is due to be registered by 10 December 2026; eSafety's July 2026 submission refers to blocking certain accounts from being recommended to child accounts. **[GUID, pending] (search; fragmentary)** — [eSafety submission](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-07/eSafety-submission-Draft-childrens-online-privacy-code-consultation-July2026.pdf)
- ICO Children's code: high privacy by default (standard 7), and when a child tries to lower a default, explain the risk and, for 10–12, suggest involving a parent or trusted adult. **[GUID, UK] (fetched, via standards 4 and 13 pages)** — [ICO standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)
- Evidence on comparison: see Hanus & Fox and Sailer & Homner in section 1. **[PEER]** — [dblp record](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ce/HanusF15)

### Inferences
- **Friend finding:** no search by name, no directory, no "people you may know", no contact-book upload. Add friends only by a one-time code or QR shown in person, or a link the student sends themselves; both sides must accept. This implements "previously accepted accounts only" and "contact syncing off".
- **"Studying now" is an online-status signal.** Default it to off, make it friends-only, give it a one-tap "study invisibly" control on the timer screen, and never expose "last active" times. It should never trigger a notification to friends by default.
- **Weekly points on the friends screen:** opt-in per student, unranked (alphabetical or by most recent, not sorted by score), with the student's own trend shown first. Consider a shared class or group goal as the main social object.
- **Block, remove and report** on every friend row, silent to the other party, with a short confirmation of what happens next. Even without chat, a report route is needed for display names or avatars; restricting names to a vetted format and avatars to a fixed set removes most of the risk.
- **Under-14 parent approval** for friends is consistent with the guidance; for 14–17 apply the same private defaults without requiring a parent.
- With no chat, no user content and no stranger discovery, the main residual risks are social pressure and exclusion, not contact harm.

### Gaps
- I did not find eSafety or 5Rights text that specifically addresses presence indicators in learning apps, or default rules for friend suggestions; the mapping from "activity hidden" to "studying now" is my inference.
- The primary guideline wording on online status was not seen; one search summary mentioned read receipts and activity visibility but not "online status" by name.
- No study was found on the effect of seeing peers "studying now" on adolescent motivation or stress.

## 5. Privacy and trust cues for children and parents

### Takeaway
The digital consent age in Bulgaria is 14, parental consent below that must be verified with "reasonable efforts" proportionate to risk, and privacy information for children should be short, layered and shown at the moment a feature is switched on. The camera timer needs the most care: camera access should be off by default, and the feature must stay strictly at presence detection, because inferring attention or emotion from a face in an education context is a prohibited AI practice in the EU since 2 February 2025.

### Cited Findings
**Consent age and parental consent**
- GDPR Article 8: where consent is the legal basis for an information society service offered directly to a child, it is valid only from 16, or a lower national age not below 13; below that, consent must be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, and the controller must make reasonable efforts to verify this, taking available technology into account. **[REG] (recalled; standard confirmed in search)** — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- Bulgaria set the age at 14 in its Personal Data Protection Act amendments in force since 2 March 2019 (Article 25c / 25в); under 14, consent is valid only if given by the parent exercising parental rights or the guardian. **[REG] (search, multiple law-firm sources; official Bulgarian text not read)** — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/bgr/legal-updates/Bulgaria-implements-GDPR-into-Personal-Data-Protection-Act); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/gdpr-guide-national-implementation-bulgaria)
- CNIL (France) recommendation 7: checking age and parental consent is an obligation of means, to be met with reasonable efforts and while respecting the child's privacy. **[GUID, FR] (search)** — [CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/recommendation-7-check-age-child-and-parental-consent-while-respecting-childs-privacy)
- EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent (section 7.1.4): what is reasonable depends on risk; in low-risk cases verification of parental responsibility by email may be sufficient, and in high-risk cases more proof may be appropriate; verification should not itself lead to excessive data collection. **[GUID] (recalled; not re-opened, and a search this session surfaced only secondary commentary)** — [EDPB Guidelines 05/2020](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-052020-consent-under-regulation-2016679_en)
- A known weakness of email-only verification is that the child can supply an address they control. **[PRAC] (search)** — [sota.io developer guide](https://sota.io/blog/gdpr-art-8-childrens-consent-age-verification-parental-authorization-digital-services-developer-guide-2026)
- Article 28 guidelines: self-declared age is not sufficient where age assurance is needed; lower-accuracy estimation does not mean lower rights impact (§33); the EU age verification solution and, later, EU Digital Identity Wallets are the reference standard. **[GUID] (2nd-hand)** — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors)

**Presenting privacy information to children**
- ICO standard 4 (transparency): "Provide 'bite-sized' explanations at the point at which use of personal data is activated" (a just-in-time notice); depending on age and risk, prompt the child to speak to an adult before activating a new use of data; use diagrams, cartoons, graphics, video and audio; dashboards should separate essential processing from optional processing the child can switch on. For 10–12, 13–15 and 16–17: offer written or audio/video versions, let the child move to simpler or fuller versions, explain consequences when a high-privacy default is changed, and provide a parallel full version for parents. **[GUID, UK] (fetched)** — [ICO standard 4](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/4-transparency/)
- GDPR Article 12(1) and Recital 58 require information addressed to a child to be in clear and plain language the child can easily understand. **[REG] (recalled)** — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)

**Camera and the AI Act**
- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f), prohibits placing on the market, putting into service or using AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons. The prohibitions apply from 2 February 2025; fines reach EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover. **[REG] (search; regulation text recalled)** — [EUR-Lex AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [WSGR](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-under-eu-ai-act.html)
- The Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices (published 4 February 2025) give as an example of the prohibited practice an education institution using an emotion recognition system to infer the interest and attention of students. **[GUID] (search/2nd-hand)** — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-part-ii)
- Scope details from the Future of Privacy Forum analysis (24 March 2026): an emotion recognition system is one identifying or inferring emotions or intentions from biometric data; physical states such as pain or fatigue are not emotions; readily apparent expressions, gestures or movements are outside the definition unless used to infer emotions; "education institutions" is read broadly, Recital 44 extends to situations related to education, and the Dutch data protection authority reads this to include online and distance learning; the medical/safety exception is narrow and does not cover general wellbeing monitoring; permitted emotion recognition is high-risk under Annex III. **[IND/expert analysis] (fetched)** — [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/)
- The Commission guidelines reportedly treat emotion detection in a commercial online language course as outside the prohibition because it is outside workplaces and education institutions. This conflicts in spirit with the broader Dutch reading above. **[GUID via PRAC] (search/2nd-hand)** — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-part-ii); contrast [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/)
- Article 28 guidelines §57b: camera and microphone access off by default for minors. **[GUID] (fetched via 5Rights analysis)** — [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Camera red lines for Climby.** Detect only "a person is present / not present". Never compute or label attention, focus quality, engagement, gaze direction, drowsiness, mood or facial expression; never store or transmit frames or derived face data; never show any camera-derived signal to parents, teachers or friends. Because Climby has teacher views, it is close enough to "education" that the broad reading could apply, so a "focus score" derived from the face would be the single highest legal risk in the product. Marketing copy should say "checks that you are at your desk", not "measures your focus".
- **Camera trust cues.** Off by default; a timer that works fully without it; a just-in-time notice at first activation in three short lines (what it does, that nothing leaves the phone, how to turn it off); a persistent on-screen indicator while the camera is active in addition to the operating system's indicator; one tap to stop. For under-14s, prompt to check with a parent first, following the ICO age-band advice.
- **GDPR position on on-device presence detection** (inference, for counsel): processing a face image only on the device, without identification, is unlikely to be special-category biometric data, but a data protection impact assessment is prudent given children plus camera plus novel technology.
- **Consent architecture.** Use consent only for genuinely optional features (friends, reminders, camera, parent/teacher sharing where applicable), each with its own unticked switch, default off. For under-14s the parent gives consent; for 14+ the student does. Core study features should rest on contract or legitimate interests, so that a refused consent does not block homework.
- **Parental verification proportionate to Climby's risk.** With no chat, no content sharing, no ads and no stranger contact, an email-based parental confirmation with a confirmation message and easy withdrawal is arguable as "reasonable efforts"; strengthen it cheaply (parent account created separately, delayed second confirmation, or teacher/school-issued link) to counter the child-controlled-email weakness. Avoid collecting ID documents, which would be disproportionate.
- **Privacy page structure.** One screen per audience (12–13, 14–17, parent), each starting with "what we never do" (no ads, no selling data, no content shown to parents or teachers, camera never leaves the phone), then a dashboard listing each optional switch and its current state.

### Gaps
- The official Bulgarian statute text and any guidance from Bulgaria's Commission for Personal Data Protection on verifying parental consent were not retrieved.
- The EDPB's dedicated guidelines on children's data have been on its work programme for years; I could not confirm whether they have been adopted as of November 2026.
- The primary Commission guidelines on prohibited AI practices were not read; whether "presence" or "gaze" is discussed there is unconfirmed, and one source reported them as still awaiting formal adoption in early 2025.
- The AI Act "digital omnibus" amendments proposed in November 2025 may have changed timelines for high-risk obligations; I did not confirm their status. The Article 5 prohibitions were already applicable and I found nothing suggesting they changed.
- Whether a consumer homework app with teacher views counts as an "education institution" context is legally unsettled; sources conflict as noted.

## 6. Parent and teacher dashboards: monitoring versus autonomy

### Takeaway
Research on adolescents consistently finds that monitoring done in an autonomy-supportive, open way is linked to more disclosure, while controlling or covert monitoring is linked to more secrecy; regulators add that the child must be told, visibly, what adults can see. Climby's "numbers, never content" design is on the right side of this, and should be completed with a student-facing view of exactly what each adult sees and a conversational, non-judgemental framing of effort data.

### Cited Findings
- Padilla-Walker et al. (2020), 945 adolescents aged 10–18: autonomy-supportive active and restrictive media monitoring were associated with more media disclosure (and, for active monitoring, less secrecy); controlling monitoring was associated with more media secrecy. Cross-sectional, adolescent self-report. **[PEER] (search)** — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4127); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/new-psychology-research-highlights-the-importance-of-talking-to-your-kids-about-media-use/)
- Lionetti et al. (2019), meta-analysis of 31 longitudinal studies (2000–2015): parental control normally declines by a low-to-medium amount across adolescence while adolescent secrecy increases. **[PEER, meta-analysis] (search)** — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/the-development-of-parental-monitoring-during-adolescence-a-meta-/)
- Cambridge Handbook of Parental Monitoring chapter (self-determination theory): autonomy-supportive communication increases the benefits of monitoring; controlling communication is counterproductive. **[PEER, review chapter] (search)** — [Cambridge Core](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-parental-monitoring-and-information-management-during-adolescence/how-can-parents-monitor-adolescents-activities-and-encourage-volitional-disclosure/8FEF769B3A2CFF4C8D992741D1E26F4A)
- Stattin & Kerr (2000): parents' knowledge of adolescents' lives comes mainly from the adolescent's voluntary disclosure, not from surveillance or control, and disclosure is what predicts good outcomes. **[PEER] (recalled)** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.36.3.366)
- Ghosh et al. (CHI 2018), "Safety vs. Surveillance": analysis of children's and teens' reviews of parental control apps found them overwhelmingly negative, describing the apps as overly restrictive and invasive of privacy and damaging to trust with parents. **[PEER, HCI] (recalled; exact percentages not verified)** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173698)
- ICO standard 11 (parental controls): "If you provide parental controls, give the child age appropriate information about this"; where monitoring is possible, "provide an obvious sign to the child when they are being monitored" (for example a lit icon). Age bands: 10–12, child-facing audio/video on what parents can see; 13–15 and 16–17, materials explaining the balance between parental and child privacy rights, plus parent materials on the child's privacy rights under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (Article 16). **[GUID, UK] (fetched)** — [ICO standard 11](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/11-parental-controls/)
- Article 28 guidelines: strengthen guardian control tools; involve children and guardians in risk reviews; 5Rights notes the guidelines give little differentiation by age under 18. **[GUID] (2nd-hand)** — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors); [5Rights analysis](https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Analysis-of-the-Guidelines-to-Article-28.1-of-the-Digital-Services-Act-PDF.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Make sharing visible and two-sided.** A permanent "Who can see what" screen for the student showing the exact numbers each linked parent or teacher sees, a small indicator when an adult view is linked, and a preview "this is what your parent sees". Linking should require the student's action (showing a code), never a silent attach. [ICO standard 11; Padilla-Walker]
- **Scale adult visibility down with age.** A fuller default for 12–13, a lighter weekly summary for 14–15, and student-controlled sharing for 16–17, reflecting the normal decline in parental control [Lionetti] and the consent age of 14.
- **Present effort, not judgement.** Weekly minutes, tasks completed and study days, shown as the student's own trend. No comparison with classmates or siblings, no red/green ratings, no "at risk" labels, no real-time "studying now" feed for parents, no alert when a day is missed. Real-time visibility is the surveillance end of the spectrum the studies link to secrecy.
- **Help the adult respond in an autonomy-supportive way.** Pair each summary with one conversation prompt ("Ask what made Thursday's session work") rather than an instruction to enforce.
- **Teacher view:** class-level aggregates by default, with individual numbers limited to tasks the teacher set; never out-of-school timing detail (for example late-night study times), which reveals home life.
- **Keep the red line** that no adult ever sees content, camera-derived data, friends list or friends' data; state it in both the student and parent privacy pages.
- Risk to flag: if parents reward or punish based on streak or minutes, the product's numbers become a performance-contingent external reward delivered at home [Deci et al., section 1], and students may game the timer. Parent guidance should say this plainly.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specific to digital parental surveillance tools and adolescent outcomes was found; the adolescent monitoring evidence is largely cross-sectional and from North America and Western Europe, not Bulgaria.
- I found no study of how showing effort metrics (minutes, streak days) to parents or teachers affects adolescent motivation or the parent-child relationship.
- UNICEF and OECD guidance (for example the OECD Recommendation on Children in the Digital Environment, 2021) and Common Sense Media's parental-control research were not retrieved this session.
- The Ghosh et al. and Stattin & Kerr findings are from memory and should be checked against the papers before being quoted.
